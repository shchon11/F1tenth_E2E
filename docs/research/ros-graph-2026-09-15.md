# The ROS 2 graph as the system boundary — 2026-09-15

Branch `feat/ros-graph`, base `0b78111`. CPU only; no GPU was taken.

`policy_node` was the whole deployment: the observation builder, the actor, the iLQR plan tracker,
the grip and clearance arms, the traction guard and `/drive`, in one process. This splits it in two
with an eight-float plan on a topic between them, so that the ROS graph is where the system's parts
meet — and then measures whether that cost anything.

**The headline: it cost nothing measurable.** Over 1920 published commands, across every arm the
graph can run, the traction guard on and off, a simulator bag and a real car bag, the split graph's
`/drive` differs from the old node's by **0.000e+00** in steering and speed.

---

## 1. What the split is

```
sensor source ──►  policy_node  ──/f1sim/plan──►  controller_node  ──►  /drive
                   ObsBuilder                     PlanTracker
                   + actor                        + arm + traction guard
```

`f1sim_interfaces/Plan` carries the raw normalized action (six curvature knots, two speed targets),
the checkpoint that produced it, a sequence number, and a header stamped with **the `/scan` the plan
was computed from**. That last field is load-bearing: `controller_node` keeps the last few scans'
sensor snapshots and tracks each plan against the snapshot its stamp names, so a plan that arrives
late cannot be paired with the next scan's measured speed. A plan that arrives *before* its own scan
is held for one scan rather than mispaired.

The plan is arm-agnostic. Nothing on the wire knows which arm the controller runs, which is what
lets one recorded plan stream be replayed through `legacy`, `fixed_low`, `+clearance` or the guard
and stay comparable — and what lets a published baseline that emits steering and speed directly
bypass the whole runtime, which is the only honest way to compare whole systems.

What makes the parity structural rather than lucky is `f1sim_ros/deploy.py`: the arm installers, the
LiDAR geometry constants and `SensorIntake` — the `/odom` + `/sensors/imu` reader — are one
implementation that both nodes call. The policy needs the IMU mean for its observation and the
controller needs its gyro-z for the tracker and its accelerometer for the guard; those are the same
numbers computed the same way from the same samples, by construction.

## 3. One observation builder

The observation is the contract between training and the car, and it was shared by convention:
`learn/obs.py` held `ObsBuilder` for the node and `gym_env` wrote the same arithmetic out again for
the batch. A drift between them does not crash; the policy simply drives on an input it was never
trained on. The attitude scale, for instance, was `ObsSpec`'s default on one side and a literal
`0.35` on the other, with nothing making them equal.

Now every arithmetic step from sensor value to observation element is a function in `learn/obs.py`
(`resample_ranges`, `norm_scan`, `norm_imu`, `norm_att`, `norm_speed`, `ATT_SCALE`) that both sides
call, and the composition is checked by execution: `F1VecEnv.message_inputs(i)` states a simulator
step as the fields the graph's topics carry — `LaserScan.ranges`, the `Imu` samples, `Odometry`'s
wheel speed, roll and pitch from the orientation, and nothing privileged — and
`ObsBuilder.build_message` turns one into an observation exactly as `policy_node` does.

`test_obs_identity.py`, for a legacy, a recurrent and a scan-channel checkpoint: scan, proprio, the
**action**, the recurrent hidden state and the decayed occupancy channel all bit-identical
(0.000e+00), once both sides have seen the same `(scan_stack − 1) · scan_stride` frames. The one
place the two deliberately differ is pinned as a test rather than left to be discovered: the node
saturates a non-positive range and the 65.533 m miss sentinel the real `urg_node` emits, which the
simulator never produces.

Batched PPO cannot run through the topics — 258 environments at 40 Hz against one car at a fifth of
real time — and the docs say so. This is what holding the contract structurally looks like instead.

## 4. Evaluation over the graph

`f1sim_ros eval` scores one suite cell with the boundary in the middle of the measurement loop. It
owns the environment `prepare_cell` builds for that cell and scores with `run_cell` — the same two
functions `benchmark run` calls — so the comparison is between two action sources, not two
implementations of a benchmark.

### Throughput (CPU, one learner, suite v2.1, speed cap 9 m/s, arm `fixed_low`)

| | |
| --- | --- |
| simulator step, CPU eager, 1 env | ~30 ms |
| policy inference, 1081 beams × 6 frames | 3–8 ms typical (240 ms worst, under contention) |
| tracker + arm | 25–55 ms |
| **graph eval, sync** | **8.4–10.0 steps/s, 0.21–0.25× real time** |
| a 3-lap S cell (553 steps) | 55 s wall; batched at one learner, 37 s |
| the whole of suite v2.1 (144 cells × 8 trials) | of the order of a day per system |

Measured while the machine was running three of worker 18's CPU benchmark jobs (load average ~30),
so these are a floor rather than a best case. A free-running graph on this desk at the car's own
0.25 s staleness timeouts holds about 16 Hz when the machine is otherwise idle and about 8 Hz under
that load — at which point the controller's watchdog starts braking between plans, which is the
correct behaviour and which `system_check` now flags (a `/drive` rate well *above* nominal is the
brake firing, not a healthy command stream).

### Graph against batched, three cells

Sync, with the simulator's per-car tracker calibration pinned to the controller's nominal numbers
(see §5). Each row is one trial, because a graph has one `/drive`.

| `S:gen:competition:0:1.15379:4401` | lap, 17.200 s | lap, 17.200 s | **same** | +0.02 % | −0.01 % |

Repeatability, which is what makes those numbers readable:

| | runs | progress [m] | spread |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| graph, `S:…:0.73423:4401` | 3 | 52.088, 51.966, 51.961 | **0.24 %** |
| batched, any cell | 3 | bit-identical | 0 |
| graph, `A:real:map16x07:0.73423:4401` (the three that hit) | 3 | 15.047, 14.472, 14.467 | **3.95 %** |

So: the graph-vs-batched difference on the two S cells (0.02–0.14 %) is **smaller than the graph's
own run-to-run variation** (0.24 %), and on the A cell the batched result (14.537 m) sits inside the
range the graph produces by itself (14.467–15.047 m). The one outcome disagreement is a threshold
cell — the learner either squeezes past the obstacle or clips it — where a sub-percent difference in
approach decides it. The suite runs eight trials per cell for exactly that reason; a graph cell is
one.

### Where the residual comes from, measured

It is not the transport and it is not the arm. It is that **a node joining a sensor stream has no
history**. `env.reset()` fills the six-frame scan stack with the spawn scan and rolls one stepped
scan into it; a node that has just started fills all six frames with the first scan it sees. Those
are two different histories of the same run, neither wrong.

The first commands of `S:…:0.73423:4401`, graph against batched:

| step | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 8 | 11 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| \|Δsteer\| [rad] | 5.5e-3 | 9.9e-3 | 5.5e-3 | 4.0e-3 | 2.9e-3 | 2.9e-3 | 1.7e-3 | 1.1e-3 |
| \|Δspeed\| [m/s] | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

The steering difference decays as the stack fills — and the speed command is identical throughout
(worst 2.4e-4 m/s over the first 39 steps), because at these speeds the tracker's acceleration is at
its bound and the predicted speed does not depend on the steering. After the warm-up the
observations are bit-identical (§3), but by then the two cars are in slightly different places and
the closed loop keeps them there.

**Conclusion.** Graph-vs-batched parity on a cell is an *outcome-level* claim, reported with the
run-to-run variation beside it. Trajectory-level parity is `test_graph_parity.py`, which holds the
sensor stream fixed and gets 0.000e+00.

## 5. Two things the graph does not know, and one it now checks

`gym_env._calibrate_tracker` draws a per-car command latency and actuator calibration for every
episode — ±20 ms of residual latency, ±0.01 rad of servo offset, ±4 % / ±3 % of gain — even with
randomisation off, and the **batched** runner's tracker is given them. `controller_node` has only
the nominal numbers, which is the real car's situation exactly: on the car they are parameters
somebody measured. `eval`'s `pin_calibration` overrides the simulator's draw with the controller's,
which is what isolates "the graph" from "the graph does not know this car's calibration"; the row
records which it was, and the numbers above are all pinned.

The `speed_cap` is the sharper one. The policy reads it as an **observation channel**, so a graph
capped at 4 m/s scoring a suite that declares 9 is a different policy answering a different
question, and every number would be wrong with nothing looking wrong. `controller_node` publishes
the cap, the arm, the friction, the traction state and its whole tracker calibration on
`/f1sim/controller/diag`, and `eval` reads them back and refuses a mismatch. The diagnostics topic
exists because of this: the graph has to be able to say what it is.

The third check is about the harness rather than the graph. Both nodes measure freshness against
the wall clock, because on a car that is the only clock that means anything. A CPU simulator steps
at a fifth of real time, so a scan legitimately arrives hundreds of milliseconds after the last one,
and the graph would (correctly) call it stale, clear the policy's observation history and its
episode memory on every frame, and score a policy that never sees motion. That failure is invisible
in the numbers — it looks like a bad policy. `graph_eval.launch.py` therefore raises the timeouts,
and `eval` refuses the run if the measured step period gets close to what the controller reports.

## 6. Bags, datasets and "is everything there?"

`config/record.yaml` is the record profile — exactly the topics the graph needs, with the rate each
should arrive at, whether it is required, whether it is simulator-only, and why it is there. One
list, three consumers: `ros2 bag record`, `bridge_node record:=<dir>`, and `system_check`. A
simulator run and a car run therefore produce interchangeable bags, which is what makes
`learn/bagdata.py` a single tool rather than two.

`learn/bagdata.py` rebuilds, for every `/scan`, the observation `ObsBuilder` would have built, and
pairs it with the `/drive` that answered it, the pose and the episode. It says out loud what it
cannot know: the previous-action channels are only in the bag when `/f1sim/plan` was recorded (or
when a direct-action run's `/drive` inverts back into one), and a `/f1sim/reset` starts a new
episode because a history stitched across one describes a run that never happened.

**A correctness fix fell out of this.** `calib/bagread.py` multiplied `linear_acceleration` by g
unconditionally — right for the 22 car recordings, and 9.81× wrong for a simulator bag on the same
topic. It is detected from the gravity vector now (`accel_scale_for`, the same rule
`deploy.SensorIntake` applies live) and recorded per topic. Measured: the car bag reads 9.80665 with
a median |a| of 10.26 m/s², the simulator bag reads 1.0 with 8.95 m/s². `replay_traction.py` on
`20260826-173704` is unchanged (3/3 must-catch, 0 false alarms).

## 7. What running it found

Two bugs that no import-level test could have seen, both now covered:

* **`traction:=off` killed the controller.** A `LaunchConfiguration` is a string and `launch_ros`
  guesses a type from it with YAML rules; YAML 1.1 reads `off` as the boolean false, so the
  deployment default arrived at a STRING parameter as `False` and the node refused to start. Every
  argument that reaches a node is now a typed `ParameterValue`, and `test_graph_launch.py` evaluates
  each launch description and checks the resolved type against what the node declares.
* **a `set` of scan stamps, trimmed by slicing.** `eval` keeps the stamps of the scans it has
  published so it can tell the command that answers one from the controller's watchdog brake. A set
  drops keys in hash order, so the trim removed, half the time, the scan being waited on: the run
  stalled at the first trim and looked exactly like a graph that had stopped answering. Insertion
  ordered and trimmed oldest first now, with a test that the newest key always survives.

And three smaller things: a bag whose recorder was killed before `metadata.yaml` was written cannot
be opened by anything, and rosbag2's "No storage could be initialized" reads as "this is not a bag"
— both readers now name the real problem and the fix (`ros2 bag reindex`, which does recover it),
the recorder registers its close with `atexit`, and `bridge_node` takes SIGTERM as well as SIGINT.
`system_check` flags a topic well above its nominal rate, not only below. And `/sensors/imu` is a
`vesc_msgs/VescImuStamped` on the car and a `sensor_msgs/Imu` from the simulator bridge — an edge
that has never connected in either direction, which is harmless only because the attitude reaches
the graph on `/sensors/imu/raw`; `eval_node` does not publish it.

## 8. What is still not true

* **Nothing here has driven a physical vehicle.** `steer_gain` is 1.0 because the two recorded servo
  configurations have opposite polarity, and the traction guard is `off` because it has only ever
  been validated by replay.
* **The graph is not a leaderboard.** One learner, a fifth of real time; suites are scored batched,
  and `benchmark report` refuses a graph row, correctly.
* **The live graph is not the replay.** Two processes receive `/scan` independently, so a plan
  occasionally overtakes its own scan. That is held for one scan and the residual is counted in
  `plan_unmatched` on the diagnostics; it is not zero, and the 0.000e+00 above is the deterministic
  replay, not a claim about DDS.
* **The rolling history buffers are still two implementations.** The env's are fused into its
  compiled step. The encodings are shared and the composition is tested, which is the property that
  matters, but it is not the same object.
